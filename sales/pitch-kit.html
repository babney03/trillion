Outreach kit · local business

The already-built pitch

Most cold emails ask for a meeting to discuss building something. Yours doesn't have to — the site already exists and they can look at it in one click. That single difference is what gets this read instead of binned. Everything below is built around it.

01 — Why this works

You're not selling. You're showing.

An owner can smell a pitch in half a line. What they can't dismiss as easily is a finished thing with their own business name on it, made by someone who clearly looked at them first.

So the email does three things in order: proves you looked, hands over the link with nothing to agree to, then states the price plainly so they're not braced for a sales call. No "quick chat", no "hop on a call", no calendar link. The link does the selling.

02 — Subject lines

Short, specific, obviously written by a person

No capitals, no exclamation marks, nothing that reads like a campaign. The best one names the business or the problem.

  • Use this one I built [Business] a website Flatly strange in a good way. It's not an offer, it's a statement, and the only way to resolve it is to open the email. Works for any trade.
  • Nobody knows you're open for dinner Problem-first. Devastating when it's true and specific — use it where you've spotted a real gap. Needs rewriting per business.
  • Made something for [Business] — link inside Softer. Good if the blunt version feels too forward for your area.
  • Your reviews are better than your website Only where it's clearly true. It's a compliment first, which stops it reading as an insult.
  • Quick one about [Business]'s booking page Lowest-key option. Lower open rate, but almost never marked as spam.
03 — The email

The main one

Roughly 180 words. Everything in highlight gets swapped per business. Don't lengthen it.

Cold email — first contact~180 words 
Subject: I built [Business] a website

Hi [Name],

I'm local to [town] and ended up on your Google listing [looking for somewhere to eat / after a storm took half my fence down]. Two things stood out: your reviews are genuinely excellent, and [the specific gap — e.g. nothing online makes it obvious you're open Wednesday to Sunday evenings].

So I built you a website. It's finished — you can look at it right now:

[LINK]

[One sentence on the single best thing it does — e.g. It opens with a panel that tells people whether you're open right now, including the gap between 2 and 4, so nobody sees "closed" and goes elsewhere.] It's built for a phone first, because that's where nearly all of this traffic comes from.

No catch and nothing to sign up for. I built it on spec because showing beats describing. If you want it, I'll put your real photos and [menu / project shots] in and have it live inside a week — $850, one time. Hosting, domain and changes after that are $35 a month if you want me to handle them. If it's not for you, ignore this and I won't chase you.

The link stays up either way.

05 — Follow-ups

Two, then stop

Most replies to cold outreach come from the second and third message, not the first. But three total is the limit — past that you're the thing they complain about.

Follow-up 14–5 days later · same thread 
Hi [Name] — just making sure this didn't get buried.

The site's still up: [LINK]

If you only look at one thing, make it [the single best feature — e.g. the panel at the top that shows whether you're open right now].

Happy to leave it at that if it's not for you — just say so and I'll stop.

[Your name]
Follow-up 2 — the close-out10 days later · highest reply rate 
Hi [Name] — I'll take the link down at the end of the week and leave you alone.

If you ever want it, it's [LINK] and my number's below.

Either way — [genuine specific wish, e.g. good luck with patio season].

[Your name]
[Phone]

The close-out pulls the most replies of the three, because it removes the pressure and gives a deadline at the same time. Mean it — actually stop after this one.

06 — Pricing

Put the number in the email

Hiding the price to "get them on a call" is why most of these go unanswered. An owner who sees a number can decide in ten seconds, and the ones who reply are already half sold.

These are my suggestion, not your price.

$850 is pitched where a small local business will say yes without a board meeting, and high enough not to read as amateur. Below about $500 owners assume it's a template farm; above about $1,500 they'll want a portfolio and references you don't have yet. Raise it once you've got three live sites and a testimonial — the work doesn't change, the proof does.

When they say it's too expensive

Don't discount. Shrink the scope or point at the maths: one extra table a month, or one extra job a year, covers it. If they still won't, take the care plan off and leave the one-time price alone — dropping your build price teaches them the first number wasn't real.

07 — Before you send

Swap these every time

  • [Name]The owner's actual name. "Hi there" halves your reply rate. It's usually on the About page, their Facebook, or the Google listing.
  • [the specific gap]The one real observation. This is the whole email — it's the proof you didn't blast 500 of these. Never reuse the same line twice.
  • [one sentence on the best thing]One feature, in their language, phrased as what a customer gets. Not a list.
  • [LINK]The live site. Check it opens on your own phone before you send.
  • $850 / $35Your numbers, in your currency.
Canadian anti-spam law — worth two minutes

CASL covers commercial email in Canada and the penalties are not small. The route that fits what you're doing: emailing an address the business has conspicuously published (on their site or listing), where it isn't marked as refusing unsolicited mail, and your message relates to their business. That's the published-address provision, and it's the reason this works where mass consumer email wouldn't.

To stay on the right side of it regardless: use your real name, include a working phone number and your town or mailing address in every message, make opting out trivial ("say so and I'll stop"), and honour it immediately. Keep a simple list of anyone who says no. This is practical guidance, not legal advice — if you scale past a few dozen a week, read the CRTC's own CASL page.

Swap the placeholders, send three a day rather than thirty a week, and keep a note of who you contacted and when. Three messages per business, then stop.
